# How we write: Sam Parr's copywriting guide

This guide decides how a journal post is written. The journal brief decides what a post may say: the facts, the sources, the OPS claims, the banned words. When the two seem to disagree about how to write, this guide wins.

## The order of work

Sam Parr writes in a fixed order, and the journal follows it.

1. **Headline first.** Write the headline before anything else. It is the plan: headlines, not outlines.
2. **Description second.** Write one or two sentences that say what the article is about. That becomes the subtitle. If the description will not come, the thinking is not done yet. Clear writing starts with clear thinking.
3. **Draft fast.** Get every thought on the page. Do not polish while drafting.
4. **Step away, then edit.** Reread the draft cold before changing it. The writing gets good in the edit, not in the draft.
5. **Cut.** Every word has to be necessary. Most drafts spend their first quarter warming up and their last quarter winding down, so check both and cut. Never pad a piece to reach a length.
6. **Fix the first sentence.** It decides whether anyone reads the second.
7. **Build the slippery slope.** Make every sentence pull the reader into the next one.
8. **Make it simple.** Simple words, short sentences, short paragraphs.
9. **Go back to the headline.** Write 25 of them, then let readers pick the one they would click.

## Headlines

- **Plain English.** Write the headline the way you would tell a friend what the article is about. Someone who reads only the headline should know what the article is about.
- **Three to ten words.** The journal sets titles in capitals. That is typography, not permission to shout or to get cute.
- **Clear beats clever.** No riddles, puns, slogans or sayings that only make sense after reading the article. The test: say it out loud to an owner at the supply yard. If the answer is "what does that mean?", it fails.
- **A promise with a gap.** Tell the reader what they get, and leave the how for the article. Curiosity, never confusion.
- **It carries the piece.** Most people read the headline and nothing else. If the headline fails, the article fails with it.
- **Write 25.** The first handful are the obvious ones. Keep going past them, then show the list to readers and take the one they would click.
- **Do not bury the lead.** The most important thing goes first: in the headline, then again in the first sentence.

Calibration from our own journal:

- **Works:** WORD OF MOUTH ISN'T A MARKETING PLAN. GREAT HANDS DON'T MAKE GREAT BOSSES. AI IS GRADING YOUR BID. Each says what the article is about, in words a friend would use.
- **Fails:** THE LAST PAYMENT IS DECIDED BEFORE THE FIRST POST HOLE. It is a riddle: you need the article to understand the headline. Jackson rejected it on 2026-09-16.

## The first sentence

- It grabs the reader straight away and cuts through everything else competing for them.
- Three reliable ways in: a personal story, a hint of what is coming, or a question.
- Its only job is to get the second sentence read.

## The slippery slope

- Every sentence makes the reader want the next one. The longer someone reads, the more they come to agree.
- Open a loop early and close it later. Small stories, a hint of what is coming and honest questions keep a reader moving.
- Good writing has rhythm. Most sentences are short, and the length still varies on purpose.

## Write simple

- **Grade 7 to 8.** Aim for a seventh- or eighth-grade reading level, with plain, everyday words.
- **Story and insight carry the piece.** Plain writing with a strong story and real insight beats beautiful writing with nothing to say.
- **Write like you talk.** Read it out loud. If it sounds like a textbook, a press release or a marketing department, rewrite it until it sounds like a smart friend explaining something over coffee.
- **Think first.** If a paragraph is muddy, the thought behind it is muddy. Fix the thought, then the words.

## Sentences, paragraphs and words

- **Sentences:** about 25 words or fewer, and most of them shorter.
- **Paragraphs:** two to four sentences.
- **Adverbs:** few. A strong verb beats a weak verb with an -ly word leaning on it.
- **Every word necessary.** If a sentence works without a word, the word goes.

## Before the draft goes back

- The headline is plain English, three to ten words, tells a stranger what the article is about, and readers would click it.
- The subtitle is the description: one or two sentences that say what the article is about.
- The first sentence grabs, and nothing warms up in front of it.
- Every sentence pulls into the next.
- The reading level sits around grade 7 to 8, sentences run about 25 words or fewer, and paragraphs hold two to four sentences.
- Adverbs are down to the few that earn their place.
- The first and last quarter have been checked for throat-clearing and wind-down, and cut.
- Read out loud, it sounds like a smart friend talking.
