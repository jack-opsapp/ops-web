# Sam Parr on Copywriting: A Deep-Dive Field Guide (with Emphasis on Web Page Copy)

## TL;DR
- Sam Parr's copywriting philosophy is remarkably consistent from 2015 to 2026: master "copywork" (hand-copying great sales letters), write to a 5th-to-8th grade level, build a "slippery slope" where each sentence's only job is to get the reader to the next, obsess over headlines (write 25+), and treat "forgotten text" (thank-you pages, welcome emails, About Us pages) as prime conversion real estate.
- On the landing-page vs. sales-page question, Parr's most specific and repeated position is that long-form sales pages (copy + photos + video + reviews, Amazon-listing style) out-convert minimalist "Silicon Valley" landing pages — he backs this with a documented A/B test where his plain black-text page made $3,000 vs. a designer's colorful page's $300 on equal ad spend.
- Parr has very little that is explicitly about the *homepage* as a distinct artifact; his frameworks are about sales pages, ads, emails, and articles. The best homepage guidance is reverse-engineered from pages he actually wrote (Hampton's joinhampton.com, CopyThat's About page, Trends.co), which lead with a visceral customer fear/"quiet thought," stack proof, and drive to one CTA.

## Key Findings

1. **Copywork is the foundation of everything he teaches.** Parr spent two hours a day for six months hand-copying great sales letters and authors; he still does ~10 minutes as a warm-up. This is the core of his CopyThat course.
2. **His single most repeated web-copy claim:** most people build simple landing pages that just describe the product; sales pages with long copy + images + video + social proof convert better, and tech startups leave money on the table by skipping them.
3. **The "slippery slope"** (borrowed from Joe Sugarman): the sole job of the headline is to get you to read sentence one; the sole job of each sentence is the next. This is his through-line from 2015 to today.
4. **Headlines get disproportionate effort:** write in 3–10 words, plain English, "as if describing it to a friend," write 25+ variations, and "when you have your headline written, you have spent ninety cents out of your dollar."
5. **"Forgotten text"** — thank-you pages, confirmation/welcome emails, unsubscribe pages, About Us pages — is under-invested conversion real estate, and one of his most transferable ideas for any website.
6. **"Quiet thoughts" (2026 evolution):** the highest-converting copy names the customer's unspoken, often shameful inner monologue. This is his most useful recent addition and is directly applicable to a service business.
7. **Simplicity is non-negotiable:** 7th–8th grade reading level (sometimes he says 5th, or 4th "like Hemingway"), sentences under ~25 words, kill adverbs, use the Hemingway app. Warren Buffett's average sentence length is 13.5 words (per Forbes, "How To Write Like Warren Buffett — Or Not," Mar 2, 2017: "once sentences exceed 17 words, the reader will struggle to grasp the concept easily. Buffett's average sentence length: 13.5 words").
8. **Edit by subtraction:** cut the first 25% and last 25% of a draft; "nine out of ten times the good shit comes out in editing."
9. **Homepage-specific advice is genuinely thin** — this is a real gap, not a research miss. What exists must be inferred from his live pages.

## Details

### A. Landing Page / Sales Page Copy (the core of your request)

This is the richest vein of Parr's web-copy thinking. His position is clear and has stayed consistent since ~2020.

**The central thesis (Indie Hackers, May 2020, with Courtland Allen).** Parr's own words: "Most people create simple landing pages that just describe the product. They aren't as persuasive. Sales pages have long copy + images + videos with the single purpose of making a sale… Look at any Amazon product and you will see 2000 words of copy + Photos and Videos of the product + Social proof in the form of authentic customer reviews." He believes Silicon Valley tech startups miss out by not writing these traditional-style sales pages, and he cited the Trends.co page as his own example that "covers all these elements of traditional copywriting."

**The A/B test (documented by ex-Hustle employee Brad Wolverton, newsletterexamples.co, Jan 2025).** This is the most concrete proof point Parr has. He bet a Hustle designer he could build a better landing page for a $300/yr newsletter. The designer's page had minimal copy, bright colors, and cute-but-vague phrases. In Wolverton's verbatim account: "His was longer but minimally designed—black text and a white background… we spent $1k on two days of Facebook ads, driving traffic equally to each page. The designer's page made $300. Sam's made $3k." A 10x difference. The lesson Parr draws: don't assume people won't read long ads or that a plainly-designed page won't convert.

**Short vs. long — the actual nuance (David Perell's "How I Write," Oct 2023).** Parr's rule is that articles/pages tend to work best at **under 500 words or over 2,000 words**, and to avoid the "no man's land" in between. This reconciles the apparent contradiction: he's not against short copy, he's against *medium* copy that is too long to be punchy and too short to fully persuade. Low-commitment/cheap or well-known offers can go short; expensive or unfamiliar offers need long copy (a point he also makes in CopyThat's Day 7 lesson: "Longer copy is better for unknown, expensive products. Shorter copy is better for well-known or cheaper products").

**The Neville Medhora live teardown of The Hustle's Ideation Bootcamp sales page (Sept 15, 2020; video + Google Doc).** This is the single best artifact showing what Parr and a pro copywriter add to an already-good sales page. The checklist of additions to boost conversion included:
- Add social proof and member quotes/testimonials (including a testimonial that shows a member *stopped* their current business to go all-in — a stronger signal than generic praise)
- Add accolades/credibility markers (Parr's founder bio: founder of HustleCon, Trends.co at $1M/mo, Bunk acquired by Apartment List, etc.)
- State the goal/outcome explicitly
- Include a "good idea vs. bad idea" example so prospects self-qualify
- Add an FAQ section
- Add screenshots of what's inside the product
- Use the value-equation framing: create X value, capture Y% of it (their example: an email-recommender idea creating $7B in sales, 5% = $500M, "Do it!" vs. Coachella at $100M × 5% = $5M, "Not worth it")
- Sell the transformation/time-saved ("save 10 years of heartache and time")

**AIDA is his backbone structure** (from Joe Sugarman's *Advertising Secrets of the Written Word*): **A**ttention (grab the reader), **I**nterest (give them facts), **D**esire (tell stories, share benefits), **A**ction (say exactly what to do). He applies AIDA to sales pages, ads, and cold emails alike.

**Sentence rhythm for sales copy** (Horizons Pod, 2025; MFM 552): vary sentence length — start short, build slightly longer, then deliver a longer sentence as a "punch." Read it out loud (the "voice memo test"); if it doesn't sound like a person talking, rewrite it.

### B. Homepage / Website Copy (and the honest gap)

**The gap is real.** After an exhaustive sweep, Parr has surprisingly little that explicitly addresses the *homepage* as a distinct format. He talks about sales pages, ads, emails, articles, headlines, and cold emails — not "here's how to write a homepage." If you were expecting a Parr "homepage framework," it does not exist as such. What follows is reverse-engineered from pages he personally wrote or oversaw.

**Hampton homepage (joinhampton.com) — reverse-engineered structure.** This is the most current, Parr-written web page and the best homepage model he offers. Section by section:
- **Above the fold / headline:** "If you're a founder… Stop making $50M decisions alone." Subhead: "Your personal board of ~10 vetted founders, meeting monthly in your city. Show up with your hardest challenge at noon, leave by dinner with a clear plan." Note it opens with a fear/pain, not a feature, and the CTA ("Apply") appears immediately.
- **Pain restatement:** "Lonely decisions are expensive. Stop paying that tax."
- **Proof stack:** concrete member results ("$12M SaaS — Hired VP of Engineering in 14 days"), testimonials with photos and names, then a wall of recognizable member logos/bios.
- **Founder letter (mid-page):** a first-person Sam Parr note beginning "Hey there," walking through the emotional core — "you've outrun your old circle… making $50M-level decisions alone… The real risk isn't blowing up. It's drifting into 'good enough'." Signed with his signature and headshot.
- **Objection handling:** the vetting process (6 steps), the "how it works," money-back guarantee, and a long FAQ.
- **Repeated CTA:** "Apply" / "Explore Membership" recur many times down the page.

The whole page targets **founder loneliness and fear of mediocrity** — the emotional "quiet thought," not the functional features of a membership community. The About Us page goes further, opening with a *story* ("How my partner Joe lost $100M") rather than facts, and only giving "the facts" ~60% of the way down ("OK, finally… So, this is an About Us page. I guess we should finally give the facts.").

**"Mediocrity is the enemy"** — the Hampton About page names the exact fear: "Failing… that's not my fear. Because failure is definitive and can be fast. But mediocrity? That drags on. Years of drifting. Waking up 10 years later realizing you played small… that thought keeps me up at night." This is the "quiet thoughts" principle executed on a live page.

**CopyThat About Us page** — Parr and his wife Sara built it to provide "street cred" and be relatable, heavy on personal photos (including their late dog Sid). His point: an About page is a conversion tool, not a boilerplate obligation.

### C. The "Quiet Thoughts" framework (2026 — his most important recent evolution)

From the mid-2026 MFM episode on lessons from a week in NYC ("60 minutes of ruthless business lessons from one week in NYC"): the highest-converting copy surfaces **"quiet thoughts"** — the customer's unspoken, often shameful or frustrated inner monologue. Examples given on-air:
- Weight-loss marketing for women: "I know why you always offer to take the photo when you're out with your friends." (Attributed specifically to Sam Parr.)
- Decluttering: "Does panic hit you when someone wants to come over for a play date?"

The mechanism (stated by Shaan Puri, agreed by both): "If you could describe the problem in such great detail that the person feels completely understood, they will trust you fully [on] whatever solution you speak next." Parr adds a framework: **general knowledge vs. specific knowledge**. General knowledge = the weeks you spend interviewing customers about their feelings and fears (the part "below the iceberg"); specific knowledge = how the product actually works. The resonant, counter-intuitive hooks come from *general* knowledge.

This is the single most transferable idea for a service business: interview your customers, find the shameful/unspoken thought, and name it in your headline.

### D. Foundational principles (consistent 2015–2026)

**Copywork.** His origin story and #1 recommendation. "I would get the best sales letters and the best writing that I could find… and I would literally copy them by hand for hours every single day for months." The musician analogy recurs everywhere: you learn by playing others' songs before writing your own. He sells a Gumroad swipe file of his favorite sales letters and built CopyThat entirely around this practice.

**The 2015 blog post ("How To Use Words To Get People To Do What You Want Them To Do," theantimba.com).** His earliest codified advice, still live. It opens: "Let's get one thing perfectly clear: You're reading these words because I want you to… I wrote a spell to make you read this." The advice: write to a 5th grader, one-or-two-syllable words, short sentences, few-sentence paragraphs, and the slippery slope ("the point of the first sentence is to get you to read the second sentence. And the point of the second sentence is to get you to read the third sentence"). His 5 recommendations: Neville Medhora's Kopywriting Kourse, Stephen King's *On Writing*, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*, Cialdini's *Influence*, and the Hemingway App at 5th-grade level.

**The Hustle's writing guide ("Write Like The Hustle," republished Oct 2024).** The internal 14-step process, with specifics: (1) write the headline first, 3–10 words, plain English; (2) write a 1–2 sentence description; (3) draft fast ("motion creates emotion," accept that it'll suck); (4) incubate; (5–6) edit and cut — "for most people, that means cutting the first 25% and last 25%"; (7) make the first sentence "punch the reader in the face"; (8) create the slippery slope; (9) write at 7th–8th grade level (use Hemingway); (10) short sentences (~25 words max) and paragraphs (2–4 sentences); (11) few adverbs ("the road to hell is paved with adverbs"); (12) go back and write 25 headlines, test with friends — "when you have your headline written, you have spent ninety cents out of your dollar"; (13) pick a "clicky" image; (14) copywork.

**"Bold Fast Fun" (BFF).** He has it tattooed on his arm and uses it as a writing filter (David Perell, 2023). His diagnosis of sterile writing: "That was written under some kind of fear. That was not written from a place of love and the flames of passion." His fix: 10 minutes of copywork from a bold writer (Anthony Bourdain, Felix Dennis) as a warm-up. "Nine out of ten times, the good shit comes out in editing. The first five paragraphs, just delete them."

**The five books that shaped The Hustle's voice:** Joe Sugarman's *Advertising Secrets of the Written Word*, David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising*, Gary Halbert's *The Boron Letters*, William Zinsser's *On Writing Well*, and Stephen King's *On Writing*.

### E. The CopyThat course, lesson by lesson

CopyThat ($169, or occasionally $149) is a 10-lesson email course delivered Mon–Fri over two weeks, ~30 min/day. Each day: read Sam's annotated breakdown of a great piece, then hand-copy it. Verified curriculum (primarily from dannybooboo.com, thecoursescout.com, kevinconnelly.blog):
- **Day 1 — Sentence structure/rhythm:** exemplar is Louis C.K.'s "Live at the Beacon Theater" page (launched Dec 10, 2011; sold direct from louisck.net for $5, topped $1M in sales within ~12 days and 110,000+ copies per CNN Money and The Hollywood Reporter) — "funny, punchy," relatable language.
- **Day 2 — AIDA / formatting your copy:** be crystal-clear on the exact next action. (Exemplar not documented.)
- **Day 3 — The slippery slope:** first sentence → second sentence, etc.
- **Day 4 — Keep it simple:** exemplars are Stephen King and the Dilbert Blog (Scott Adams). "Prune your sentences. Abolish adverbs. Pulverize the passive voice." Warren Buffett's shareholder letters are tied to this theme.
- **Day 5 — Storytelling / start with a story:** exemplar is the Wall Street Journal "Two Young Men" subscription letter — written by copywriter Martin Conroy in 1973, it ran 1975–2003 (28 years) and generated an estimated $2 billion; per Conroy's 2006 NYT obituary, consultant James R. Rosenfield called it "the Hamlet, the Iliad, the Divine Comedy of direct-mail letters… a longer life, to my knowledge, than any other direct mail in history." (Sam provides an annotated PDF.)
- **Day 6 — Write like you speak:** exemplar is an email-declutter service sales email (brand not documented).
- **Day 7 — Long-form copy:** when/how to use it — "Longer copy is better for unknown, expensive products. Shorter copy is better for well-known or cheaper products." (Gary Halbert's letters are course copywork, likely here.)
- **Day 8 — "Forgotten" copy:** exemplars are Groupon's old unsubscribe page (video + interactivity) and The Hustle's original welcome email (humor).
- **Day 9 — Get people to say "yes" early and often:** exemplar is Notion's sales letter/landing page.
- **Day 10 — Emotion:** "write your copy like you're writing an episode of Succession — a main character, a hero's journey, ups and downs." (No single named exemplar.)

The famous Hustle welcome email he breaks down: "What just happened was magic. You see, as you entered your email, a little bell went off in our office. And when we heard that bell, we went crazy. I just saw my head of operations, Cara, she just ran outside and hugged a guy."

## Recommendations (for a residential service-business website)

**Stage 1 — Nail the "quiet thought" before you write a word.** Interview 5–10 recent customers. Find the unspoken, slightly embarrassing thought behind hiring you (e.g., for a home service: "I'm embarrassed to have people over because of [X]," or "I'm scared I'll get ripped off by a contractor again"). Your homepage headline should name that thought the way Hampton names founder loneliness. This is the highest-leverage move and the most defensibly "Parr" thing you can do. Benchmark: if a stranger reads your headline and thinks "how did they know that?", you've got it.

**Stage 2 — Structure the homepage like a compressed sales page, not a brochure.** Above the fold: pain-based headline + one-sentence outcome + one CTA. Then: proof (specific results with numbers and names, not vague testimonials), objection handling (guarantee, process, FAQ), and a repeated CTA. Follow Parr's "forgotten text" principle: rewrite your thank-you page, your quote-confirmation email, and your About page — for a local service business, the About page (real photos of the owner/crew, the origin story) builds the trust that closes local jobs.

**Stage 3 — For your money pages (individual services / the "get a quote" page), go long, not medium.** A high-consideration, expensive, trust-sensitive purchase (which most residential services are) is exactly the case where Parr says long copy wins: problem in the customer's words → your solution → detailed proof (photos, before/afters, reviews) → risk reversal/guarantee → clear CTA. Avoid the 800–1,500-word "no man's land." Either a tight landing page for a simple/cheap offer or a full long-form page for the core service.

**Stage 4 — Apply the mechanical rules everywhere.** Headline in 3–10 words of plain English; write 25 variations and pick the one friends would click. First sentence punches. Slippery slope. 7th–8th grade reading level (run every page through the Hemingway app). Kill adverbs. Cut the first and last 25% of your draft. Read it out loud.

**Benchmarks that would change the plan:** If a long service page underperforms a short one on revenue-per-visitor in an honest A/B test (Parr's own method: equal ad spend, split traffic), keep the short one — he cares about conversion, not length dogma. If your traffic is mostly warm/referral (already trust you), you can lean shorter; cold paid traffic needs the long, proof-heavy page.

## Caveats

- **Homepage-specific guidance is inferred, not stated.** Parr has no published "homepage framework." Everything in Section B is reverse-engineered from pages he wrote. Treat it as well-grounded inference, not a direct quote.
- **His frameworks were built for newsletters, courses, communities, and info products** — not local/residential services. The "quiet thoughts," long-copy, and forgotten-text ideas transfer well; the specific examples (Amazon listings, SaaS, founder communities) do not map one-to-one to, say, a plumbing or landscaping site. Test claims and proof formats for your market.
- **Long vs. short is often overstated by third parties.** Parr's actual position is "short or long, avoid the middle," plus "match length to price/familiarity" — not "always write long." The $3k-vs-$300 anecdote is one test of one page, reported by a former employee, not a controlled study.
- **Some widely-circulated "Sam Parr quotes" are secondhand.** SEO aggregators dramatize or composite his lines. Where exact phrasing matters, go to the primaries listed above.
- **Sam vs. Shaan attribution is genuinely blurred** on co-hosted MFM content and the HubSpot masterclass. Ideas like the curiosity gap and "story as a moment of change" are more Shaan's; copywork, forgotten text, and the A/B test are clearly Sam's.
- **A note on reading level:** Parr cites 5th grade (2015), 7th–8th grade (Hustle guide), and 4th grade "like Hemingway" (Horizons) at different times. Don't over-index on the exact number — the consistent instruction is "much simpler than you think."
- **The Hustle sale price is commonly cited as "$27M" (Axios estimate, Feb 3, 2021),** but HubSpot's SEC filing valued it at ~$20.3M ($17.2M net cash plus ~$3.1M other consideration); Parr has said his total exceeded $20M. The "$27M exit" figure that appears in interview titles is Axios's estimate, not the filed price — minor, but worth knowing if you cite it.
