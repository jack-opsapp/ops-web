The Federal Trade Commission keeps a plain-English guide for homeowners about to hire someone to work on their house, and one line in it should change how you close every job: “Even if your state doesn’t require a written agreement, ask for one.” Read that from the customer's side of the kitchen table. A federal consumer agency is telling the person about to hand you a deposit that a handshake isn't enough — and the same page lists the moves scammers make, including pressure for an immediate decision and asking to be paid for everything up front or only in cash.

Look, most owner-operators who run on handshakes aren't hiding anything. The customer seemed decent, the truck sat loaded in the driveway, and pulling out paperwork felt like telling a good person you didn't trust them. That instinct comes from a good place. It also leaves you standing in somebody's backyard with nothing but your memory against theirs.

What a handshake actually costs

The bill for a handshake shows up in the middle of the job, when the homeowner remembers the railing as included and you remember it as an add. Nobody is lying — two people heard the same conversation and walked away with two different jobs in their heads. Say that railing is $2,400 of material and labor on a deck you already priced thin. You either swallow it or you fight a customer whose neighbors are the referrals you're counting on, and word of mouth only carries a shop whose jobs end clean. Whether you set railings, hang fence or paint houses, the argument sounds the same.

Then there's the law, which forgives less than the customer does. California requires a written contract for every home improvement project over $500, and its licensing board says any change to price or scope goes on a written change order that both sides sign before the change. Virginia's Department of Professional and Occupational Regulation says licensed businesses must provide a written contract for residential work, signed by both parties. Contract rules vary by state, and the FTC says so directly. California and Virginia still point the same way: if it isn't written and signed, it isn't the deal.

The homeowner already has the checklist

Here's the part most crews miss. The FTC guide tells homeowners what belongs in the contract, and California's licensing board adds the detail. Put them together and you have the checklist your customer is quietly holding you to:

  • Who you are: business name, address, phone number and license number.
  • The work, in detail: products, materials, sizes, colors, model numbers, and who does what.
  • The dates: an estimated start date and a completion date.
  • The money: the price and a written payment schedule that never runs ahead of the work, beyond the deposit.
  • Permits: who pulls them, in writing.
  • Cleanup: debris removal and special requests, like saving lumber for firewood.
  • Warranties: labor and materials, written down instead of promised in the driveway.
  • Every promise made out loud: anything you said about scope or cost on a call or a walk-through.

And no blanks. The FTC tells homeowners to make sure someone fills in every blank space, and never to sign a document that still has one.

Think of the signed agreement like instant replay in football. Nobody wins an argument with the ref by yelling louder; they win it by going to the tape. When the homeowner swears the gate hardware came with the job, you both look at the paper, and whatever it says is the call. Honestly? That isn't cold. It's fair to both of you — and a clean written estimate is also how homeowners grade your bid.

Deposits and the cancellation clock

Two more lines belong on paper because regulators already wrote them. First, the deposit. In California, the down payment on a home improvement job cannot be more than $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less, with no exceptions for special-order materials. Virginia's regulator coaches homeowners to consider no more than 10% down or $1,000, whichever is less, and no more than 30% when the job needs custom-made items or special orders. Check your state's limit and write the deposit inside it.

Second, the cancellation clock. Sell a job at the customer's kitchen table and the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule can give them three days to cancel. The rule covers door-to-door sales over $25. You have to tell the buyer about the right to cancel at the time of the sale, hand over two copies of a cancellation form, and give them a dated contract or receipt with your name and address that explains that right. The clock runs until midnight of the third business day, and Saturday counts as a business day. Sign at the house on a Friday, with no federal holiday in the way, and the customer has until midnight Tuesday to walk. If they cancel, you have 10 days to refund all their money. Our read: don't spend a cancellable deposit on materials until the clock runs out.

The rule has exceptions, including sales needed to meet an emergency, and some state laws give buyers more rights than the federal rule. The FTC lays out the full cooling-off details; check your state's version too.

An e-signature is still a signature

Here's where the fight gets easier: paper doesn't have to mean paper. The federal Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act, enacted June 30, 2000, says a signature, contract or record tied to a transaction in or affecting interstate commerce “may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form.” A customer signing on their phone in the driveway is signing. Read the statute yourself at 15 U.S.C. 7001.

Two catches. The law doesn't force anyone to accept electronic records, so keep paper ready for the customer who wants it. And it leaves the content and timing of required consumer disclosures alone — the cancellation notice still has to show up on time, and delivering a required disclosure electronically takes the customer's affirmative consent first. Then send back a signed copy they can keep and reprint, because where a law requires a written contract, an electronic record nobody can keep and accurately reproduce can lose its legal effect.

Here's what this means for you

You don't need a filing cabinet in the truck. You need one agreement and the discipline to use it on every job, including the small repair calls where handshakes hide and small jobs quietly bleed margin. Before the next estimate goes out:

  1. Build one agreement from the checklist above and check it against your state licensing board's rules on contracts, deposits and cancellation.
  2. Get it signed, on paper or on a phone, before a tool comes off the truck.
  3. Put every change to price or scope on a signed change order before the crew does the work.
  4. When you sell at the customer's home, add the cancellation notice and both copies of the form, and hold the materials order until the clock runs out.
  5. Send the customer a signed copy they can keep, every time.

Your lead hand lives by it too. If he agrees to changes on site, the change order is his job, and promoting your best hand without training him is how that step gets skipped. Get the signed change to the crew through comms your crew actually uses, whether you run roofing crews or pool service routes.

It's why we built OPS estimates the way we did: the client opens the estimate on their phone, picks the options, answers the questions you set, and signs. A handshake still matters — it's how you look a customer in the eye and promise to do the job right. The signature is how both of you remember what right meant.

Shake the hand, sign the paper, then pick up the tools.
